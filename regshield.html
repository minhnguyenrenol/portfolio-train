Five thousand breaks a day, fixed by hand, against a date that would not move.

Every trade is reported to a regulator. When a report is missing details, operations has seven business days to fix it and prove it. They worked in a spreadsheet and Jira, and the Jira licence was ending.

The design

Proven patterns first, because the design system had none for this.

One quarter, and no patterns for this density yet, so I used ones people already know. Screens and the prototype walkthrough are shared on request.

  • A personal queue: what’s mine, what’s due, what to pick up next.
  • Chips with counts and three tabs, so the size of each pile is visible.
  • A dense table grouped by the field that is wrong, largest first.
  • One person fixes, a second signs off, and every break keeps its audit history.

Leading the work

The trade-off went on the first slide, not in the appendix.

  • Put the risk of one designer, one quarter and no patterns in front of operations and engineering, and got a shared Bronze target out of it.
  • Turned “a year of open questions” into an agreed scope and a build-ready prototype in about two months.

Thirty-second version

  • Problem. About 5,000 breaks a day for US FX reporting alone, each with seven business days to fix. Operations found, fixed and evidenced them in a spreadsheet with macros and in Jira tickets. The Jira licence ended in September and an audit item was due in December, so the date could not move.
  • What I did. Took over a prototype organised by system (processing runs, data-quality categories, a counter on every menu item) that showed one trade per row with every broken field listed in a sentence. Rebuilt it around the operator's day: what's mine, what's due, what to pick up next, with breaks grouped by the field that is wrong, largest group first, and one person fixing while a second signs off. Used AI to log every session as a decision and to collect feedback pinned on the running prototype.
  • Result. Eight people (three managers, two operations analysts, two support analysts and one from compliance) scored the old way 3.2 out of 5 on ExQual: slow, error-prone, the same job over and over, and no view of progress. They scored the rebuild 4.6, in the Platinum band, against a Bronze target. Likely to recommend: 8.3 out of 10. Product and engineering got an agreed scope and a build-ready prototype in about two months: 3 user roles, 3 MVP apps, 150 features ranked must, should and could. My manager's informal view is that this would have taken about a year before; my own estimate is that AI supported about 60% of my design tasks. Both are estimates. Not live yet.
